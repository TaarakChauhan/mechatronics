Module 6 — Models and control

Dynamics & transfer functions

Dynamics describe how outputs evolve after inputs change. Transfer functions compactly represent linear time-invariant systems in the Laplace domain.

First and second order

First-order: G(s) = K/(τs+1) — exponential approach, no overshoot for step responses of the plant alone. Second-order: underdamped systems overshoot and ring; damping ratio ζ and natural frequency ωn set the shape. Settling time and overshoot are standard specs.

Identification

Apply a step or PRBS, record y(t), fit τ, K, ζ, ωn. Poor identification → pretty controllers that fail on the machine.

Algebra of blocks

Series: multiply Gi. Negative unity feedback around G: G/(1+G). Poles are roots of the denominator — they dominate stability and speed.

Worked example — first-order step

K = 2, τ = 5 s. For unit step, y(t) = 2(1−e−t/5). At t = 5 s, y ≈ 1.26 (63% of the way from 0 to 2). At t = 20 s (~4τ), essentially settled near 2.

Key terms

G(s)
Transfer function Y(s)/U(s) under zero ICs.
Pole
Denominator root shaping natural response.
Settling time
Time to enter and stay in a band around final value.

Zeros, delays, and nonminimum phase

Zeros shape transients; right-half-plane zeros (nonminimum phase) force undershoot and limit aggressiveness — common in some booster and process systems. Time delays add infinite phase lag progression and shrink margins. If your identified model needs a delay to fit, do not drop it when designing controllers.

Partial fraction expansion of G(s) reconnects poles to modal responses. Even without heavy algebra, knowing “dominant poles” explains why slow thermal modes linger after fast electrical transients die.

From specs to pole placement intuition

Settling time specs roughly constrain the real parts of dominant poles. Overshoot specs constrain damping. Use these to sketch acceptable pole regions before picking gains.

Series proportional gain moves closed-loop poles along root-locus branches. Even a qualitative root-locus sketch prevents random gain fiddling.

When identification yields a first-order-plus-dead-time model, Smith predictors or detuned PI are common process responses; motion systems usually refuse large dead times and demand mechanical redesign.

Document model units in G(s). Mixing degrees and radians is a perennial source of 2π errors.

Extended studio notes — 06 dynamics and transfer

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Specs on overshoot and settling are pole-region statements in disguise. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming second-order forms for a plant with long delay produces brittle tunings. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Identification PRBS tests should respect actuator limits and safety interlocks. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Keep units on G(s) gains; dimensionless laziness causes order-of-magnitude errors. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Bode tools ahead consume the same G(s) you identify here. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

K=1.5, τ=2 s, unit step: y(t)=1.5(1−e^{−t/2}). At t=6 s (3τ), y≈1.425, within 5% of final 1.5 — a quick field check that your τ estimate was sane.
