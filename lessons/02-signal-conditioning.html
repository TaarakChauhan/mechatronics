Module 2 — Sensing the physical world

Signal conditioning

Raw sensor outputs are often tiny, noisy, biased, or at the wrong impedance. Signal conditioning shapes them into signals a controller or ADC can use safely and accurately.

Op-amp roles

Operational amplifiers implement gain, buffering (impedance conversion), differential amplification (instrumentation amps for bridges), filtering, and simple linearisation. Protection networks (clamps, TVS, RC filters) sit at the frontier against ESD and miswiring.

Filtering and noise

Analogue low-pass filters reduce aliasing before sampling and reject RF. Digital filters follow the ADC. Ground loops, capacitive coupling, and magnetic pickup are fought with shielding, twisted pairs, differential signalling, and sensible cable routing.

Wheatstone imbalance (quantified)

For a bridge with equal resistances R and one arm changing by ΔR, the differential output is approximately Vs·(ΔR)/(4R) for small ΔR — hence microvolt-level signals needing instrumentation gain.

Figure: Typical analogue front-end stages before digitisation.

Worked example — bridge output

Vs = 5 V, R = 350 Ω, ΔR = 0.7 Ω (strain). Vout ≈ 5 × 0.7 / (4 × 350) = 2.5 mV. With instrumentation gain G = 400, ADC sees 1.0 V — a comfortable mid-scale signal on a 0–3.3 V MCU ADC after offsetting.

Watch-out PWM average voltage is not a quiet DC level unless filtered. Driving a “analogue” input with raw PWM can inject ripple into control loops.

Grounding, isolation, and PWM realities

Single-ended ADC inputs on a noisy chassis invite ground loops. Differential inputs, isolation amplifiers, and proper cable shields (grounded at one end or per EMC plan) are part of conditioning, not optional jewellery. Isolation also protects people and ADCs when measuring near mains-referenced circuits.

PWM used as a “DAC” needs an LC or RC filter whose cutoff sits below the plant’s sensitive frequencies but above the command bandwidth. In motor drives, the inductance of the windings is the filter — intentional. On a slow thermal plant, a few hertz of PWM ripple may be invisible; on a strain channel, it is a disaster.

EMC and front-end design practice

Partition analogue and digital grounds thoughtfully and connect them at a single star point unless your ADC design dictates otherwise. Route high di/dt motor cables away from millivolt bridge signals — distance is the cheapest filter.

Instrumentation amplifiers need input protection that does not destroy common-mode range. Series resistors, TVS diodes, and RC filters must be analysed together, not sprinkled independently.

Anti-alias filter order interacts with sample rate. A gentle one-pole filter may be enough if you oversample heavily and filter digitally; aggressive control loops may need sharper analogue roll-off and still respect phase lag.

For bridge sensors, ratiometric referencing (ADC reference tied to bridge excitation) cancels first-order supply drift. Forgetting ratiometric design leaves you chasing phantom strain with every supply sag.

Extended studio notes — 02 signal conditioning

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Millivolt signals beside VFD cables without differential sensing is an EMC lottery. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming PWM average equals clean DC ignores ripple that aliases into ADCs. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Bridge amplifiers with poor CMRR turn motor noise into fake strain. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Front-end sketches with protection and filter cutoffs belong in design reviews. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Digital filtering later cannot fully undo clipped, saturated front ends. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

Bridge output 2 mV, desired ADC span 2 V ⇒ gain 1000. If amplifier GBW is only 1 MHz, closed-loop bandwidth at gain 1000 may be ~1 kHz before other poles — marginal for 5 kHz mechanical vibration content. Choose an instrumentation amp with adequate bandwidth at that gain.
