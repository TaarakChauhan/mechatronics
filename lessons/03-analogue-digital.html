Module 3 — Digital information

Analogue to digital

Controllers live in discrete time and discrete amplitude. Bridging the analogue plant to digital brains requires sampling, quantisation, and conversion hardware — and an awareness of what information is lost.

Sampling and quantisation

The Nyquist–Shannon idea: to reconstruct a bandwidth-limited signal, sample faster than twice the highest frequency of interest. In control, you also need sampling fast relative to closed-loop bandwidth (rules of thumb often place sample rate 10–30× the desired bandwidth). Quantisation maps a continuous voltage to 2N levels for an N-bit ADC; LSB size = range / 2N.

ADC and DAC types (conceptual)

Successive approximation (SAR) ADCs are common on MCUs; delta-sigma excel at high resolution for slower signals; flash ADCs are fast and power-hungry. DACs rebuild analogue commands for valves and analogue drives; many “DACs” in motion are PWM + filter or digital links to drives.

Multiplexers, DAQ, DSP

A multiplexer shares one ADC across channels at the cost of sequential sampling skew. Data acquisition (DAQ) systems package conditioning, conversion, and software. Digital signal processing (DSP) applies filters, FFT-based analysis, and control algorithms on sampled streams.

Serial vs parallel communications

Parallel buses move many bits at once (short, wide cables historically). Serial links (SPI, I²C, UART, USB, Ethernet) send bits sequentially with fewer wires and better noise tolerance over distance — the modern default.

Worked example — quantisation

0–3.3 V ADC, 12-bit: 4096 levels, LSB ≈ 0.806 mV. If a sensor span maps to 0.5–2.5 V (2.0 V span), effective counts across span ≈ 2480. Quantisation step in engineering units = measurand_span / 2480.

Then vs now Parallel ADC buses on backplanes gave way to high-speed serial (LVDS, Ethernet). Edge MCUs now integrate multi-Msps ADCs beside radios and ML accelerators.

Key terms

Nyquist rate
Lower bound related to twice signal bandwidth for sampling.
LSB
Least significant bit — smallest quantisation step.
DAQ
Data acquisition hardware/software stack.

Effective number of bits and timing

Datasheet N-bit resolution is not ENOB (effective number of bits) in the field. Noise, reference drift, and clock jitter erode useful bits. A 16-bit converter in a hostile EMC environment may deliver 11–12 honest bits. Design with ENOB, not marketing bits.

Simultaneous sampling matters for power calculations and multi-axis motion snapshots. Multiplexed ADCs introduce skew; sample-and-hold banks or multi-ADC chips fix it at a cost. Timestamping with a common clock turns a bag of samples into a coherent experiment.

Sampling strategy for control

Choose sample time from closed-loop bandwidth goals, computational load, and sensor noise. Excessively fast sampling without filtering amplifies differentiated noise in PID D-terms. Excessively slow sampling adds delay and hurts margins.

Use oversampling plus averaging to gain effective resolution on slow signals — a valid ENOB strategy when noise is whitish and the process is slow. Do not average away the dynamics you intended to control.

Synchronise sampling to PWM carriers in motor drives to reduce ripple aliasing into current loops. Randomly timed samples of a PWM waveform produce mysterious current noise.

Document endianness, scaling, and calibration constants next to every networked analogue value. Silent mismatches between PLC and drive scaling are endless sources of “the gain feels wrong.”

Extended studio notes — 03 analogue digital

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Sample rate is a control parameter, not only a DAQ convenience. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming 16 bits survive industrial noise without layout work is optimistic. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Multiplexer skew can fabricate false power-factor readings in three-phase systems. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Timestamp policy should be written before the first spreadsheet export. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Networked setpoints later inherit these quantisation and delay truths. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

Closed-loop bandwidth target 20 Hz ⇒ sample ≥ 200 Hz as a starting rule of thumb. Choose 1 kHz to allow filtering overhead. Computation delay 400 µs at 1 kHz is 0.4 samples — include it in discrete design rather than pretending controllers are instantaneous.
