Module 9 · Robot mechanics and estimation

Spatial transforms and frames

Mechatronics Studio so far treated many systems as scalar loops. A temperature reaches a setpoint. A shaft speed tracks a reference. That mindset still matters. Real products also move bodies through space. A robot arm, a camera on a gimbal, a pick head on a gantry, and a mobile base all need a shared language for where things are and which way they point.

Position, orientation, pose

Position answers where a point sits relative to a frame. Orientation answers which way a body is twisted relative to a frame. Pose combines both. When a datasheet says a gripper is at a pose, it claims a full placement, not only an XYZ triple.

Why frames multiply

Every sensor and actuator secretly owns a frame. An encoder measures a joint angle in the joint frame. A force torque sensor reports wrenches in its flange frame. A depth camera sees points in the camera optical frame. If software assumes those frames are identical, calibration bugs appear as mysterious offsets.

Name frames early. Typical names include world or map, base, shoulder, elbow, wrist, tool, and camera. Consistency beats cleverness.

Learning outcomes Explain why mechatronic products need named frames. Distinguish position, orientation, and pose. Compose a simple chain of transforms in words and in a 2D sketch. Relate frames to ROS style TF trees and to camera to tool calibration.

Composition intuition

If you know where the camera sits on the robot and where an object sits in the camera view, you can reason about where the object sits in the robot base. You multiply transforms in a careful order. The algebra appears in open robotics courses. The engineering habit is simpler: draw the chain, label each arrow, and refuse to add vectors from mismatched frames.

Connection to Module 8

When the ROS 2 lesson mentioned transforms, this is the missing foundation. A TF tree is a living graph of these named poses, updated as joints move.

Worked scenario · vacuum gripper approach

Vision reports the box center 0.4 m ahead of the camera and 0.05 m to the left. The camera sits 0.25 m above the tool flange and looks slightly down. Without a frame diagram, a programmer might command the tool to the camera numbers and miss the box. With a frame diagram, the team converts camera measurements into base coordinates, then into a tool approach pose with a safe offset along the tool Z axis.

Studio lab idea On paper or in a simulator, define base, camera, and tool frames for a desk robot. Measure or assume one fixed camera to tool transform. Given a fake object pose in camera coordinates, compute a tool approach pose with a 30 mm standoff.

Key terms

Frame
A named coordinate system attached to a body, sensor, or world origin.
Pose
Position plus orientation of a body relative to a frame.
Transform
The mapping that takes coordinates from one frame into another.
TF tree
A graph of related frames that updates as the mechanism moves.
Calibration
Measuring and storing fixed transforms between sensors, tools, and robot links.
Wrench
A combined force and torque expressed in a frame.
