Module 3 — Digital information

Data presentation & DAQ

Data that nobody trusts or can interpret is wasted silicon. Presentation, recording, calibration, and traceability turn samples into decisions.

Displays and recording

From LEDs and seven-segment displays to industrial HMIs and web dashboards, the human factors matter: update rate, units, alarms, and trends. Recording ranges from circular SD logs to historian databases with compression.

DAQ architecture

A clean architecture separates: field sensors → conditioning → conversion → timestamping → buffering → storage/analysis → visualisation. Clock sync (PTP, IRIG, or simple host time) matters when correlating multi-axis motion or vibration.

Calibration and traceability

Calibration compares a device to a reference and adjusts mapping coefficients. Traceability chains calibrations back to national standards. In regulated industries, as-found/as-left data and due dates are part of the product, not paperwork afterthoughts.

Key idea A number without units, timestamp, and uncertainty is incomplete engineering information.

Worked example — two-point calibration

A transmitter should read 0 kPa → 4 mA and 100 kPa → 20 mA. Measured: 0 kPa → 4.2 mA, 100 kPa → 19.6 mA. A linear correction maps raw current to engineering units; after adjustment, verify mid-scale (50 kPa) for nonlinearity.

Then vs now Paper chart recorders gave way to digital historians; calibration certificates became digital twins of the instrument asset with QR-linked history.

Human factors and audit trails

Alarm floods train operators to ignore alarms. Good presentation designs prioritise, shelve intelligently, and show first-out causes. Trends with proper scaling beat raw scrolling tables for process insight.

Regulated environments require audit trails: who changed a setpoint, when, from what to what. That is data presentation for accountability, not only for control. Time sync across historians and controllers keeps those stories coherent.

Uncertainty and traceability culture

Display resolution should not exceed meaningful precision. Showing seven decimals from a noisy 12-bit channel trains distrust or false confidence. Match display digits to uncertainty.

Calibration stickers without as-found data hide whether the process was out of tolerance before adjustment. Keep the history; quality systems depend on it.

For DAQ, separate configuration management (channel lists, gains) from run data. Losing the configuration makes the dataset nearly worthless months later.

Operator colour conventions (alarm red/amber) should be consistent across HMIs. Accessibility and lighting conditions in plants argue for shape/text redundancy, not colour alone.

Extended studio notes — 03 data presentation

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Operators act on what they see; misleading HMIs create unsafe actions. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming more digits means more truth trains overconfidence. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Historians without sync produce causal fairy tales. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Traceability chains turn arguments into evidence during audits. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Digital twins later need the same unit and time hygiene. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

A two-point span calibration shifts 4.2–19.6 mA back toward 4–20 mA. Mid-scale verification at 12 mA expected for 50% reveals a 0.3 mA bow — nonlinearity that two-point calibration cannot remove. Decide whether to accept, linearise with a third point, or replace the transmitter.
