Module 1 — Foundations

Measurement and control

Mechatronic products almost always contain a measurement chain and often a control chain. Separating the ideas helps you debug: if the display is wrong, is the sensor bad, the conditioning wrong, or the control law fighting a good measurement?

The measurement chain

A typical chain is: physical quantity → sensing element → transduction to an electrical signal → conditioning (amplify, filter, linearise) → digitisation → processing → display, log, or control input. Each stage adds delay, noise, and possible nonlinearity.

Figure: Measurement chain from physical quantity to useful information.

Sequential vs continuous control

Sequential (logic) control advances through steps when conditions are met: clamp → weld → release. Interlocks and timers dominate. Continuous (regulatory) control holds a variable near a setpoint with ongoing correction: temperature, speed, pressure. Real machines mix both — a bottling line is mostly sequential, but each filler head may have continuous level or flow loops.

PLC as industrial controller (intro)

A programmable logic controller is a rugged computer specialised for industrial I/O and cyclic scanning of logic. Historically ladder diagrams mimicked relay panels; modern PLCs also run structured text and function blocks. Mentally model a PLC as: read inputs → solve programme → write outputs → repeat, with deterministic scan timing. Module 5 develops this fully.

Key idea Measurement quality limits control quality. No PID tuning repairs a sensor that saturates, aliases, or drifts outside the calibration.

Worked example — sequential + continuous

An oven door cycle is sequential: close door → purge → heat → soak → cool → open. During “heat” and “soak,” a continuous PID loop holds 180 °C. If the thermocouple fails open, the controller may command full heat — so the sequential layer must include a sensor-health interlock that aborts the continuous loop.

Then vs now Discrete PLCs and separate PID controllers were often wired as islands. Today soft-PLCs, PAC architectures, and OPC UA unify sequential and continuous control on shared networks — same concepts, denser integration.

Timing and integrity in mixed systems

When sequential and continuous control share one controller, define task rates. Logic may scan at 20 ms; a pressure loop may need 5 ms; diagnostics may run at 1 s. Priority inversion — a slow HMI script blocking a fast loop — is a common industrial bug in soft platforms.

Integrity means knowing when data is stale. A measurement chain should expose quality bits: sensor OK, out of range, last-good-value age. Control code that treats NaN or frozen values as truth will wind up integrators or command unsafe actuator moves.

Watch-out “Hold last value” on sensor fail is sometimes acceptable for brief glitches and catastrophic for heaters and motion. Specify the fail policy per loop.

Scaling, units, and loop naming

Industrial programmes collapse when tags mix raw counts, milliamps, and engineering units without discipline. Adopt a rule: convert to engineering units at the boundary once, then compute in physical units. Document the scaling equation next to the channel: EU = (raw − offset) × gain.

Name loops by controlled variable and equipment: TIC-101 for temperature indicating controller on unit 101. Sequential steps should reference interlock conditions that include measurement quality, not only raw comparisons.

Continuous controllers need explicit modes: manual, auto, cascade. Bumpless transfer requires tracking the manual output so switching does not bump the actuator. These details separate textbook PID from plant PID.

For PLCs introduced here, remember scan time is a real delay. A 20 ms scan is a 10 Hz-ish update with jitter. Fast hydraulic servo loops usually leave the PLC for dedicated motion controllers — an architectural choice you can already justify with timing maths.

Extended studio notes — 01 measurement and control

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Scan time, filtering delay, and actuator slew limits all consume your stability budget before Kp is even chosen. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming analogue values are always “fresh” without timestamps is how stale temperature readings overheat ovens. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Sequential logic that ignores continuous-loop faults will clamp and heat forever. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Tag naming and unit discipline are safety features when night-shift technicians inherit your code. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. PLC lessons deepen the scan model; this lesson sets why it matters. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

A packaging cell needs a glue-bead temperature loop at 200 ms and photo-eye logic at 5 ms. Putting both in one slow scanned routine causes missed eyes. Split priorities or hardware so fast discrete events are captured while the thermal loop runs slower — architecture follows timing.
