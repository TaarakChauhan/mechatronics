Module 9 · Robot mechanics and estimation

Discrete time control on embedded targets

Module 6 treated PID in the continuous story. Controllers on MCUs and PLCs run on clocks. They sample errors, update outputs, and wait for the next tick. Good gains on paper fail when the sample period is too slow, when interrupts stretch, or when the integrator keeps summing during saturation.

Sampling and delay

Each sample holds a measurement and a decision. Holding introduces delay. Delay eats phase margin. A common studio rule is to sample several times faster than the closed loop bandwidth you want, then verify on hardware with step tests. Exact factors vary by plant. The habit of measuring matters more than memorising a single number.

Learning outcomes Explain how continuous PID becomes a difference equation on a fixed sample period. Choose a sample rate relative to closed loop bandwidth with a practical rule of thumb. Implement anti windup and derivative filtering ideas in firmware structure. List timing faults that look like bad gains.

Difference form (conceptual)

A discrete controller stores past errors and past outputs. Proportional action uses the current error. Integral action accumulates error times the sample period. Derivative action looks at error change over the sample period, usually through a filtered measurement so noise does not become a thruster command.

Write the update as clear states in code. Name error, integral, derivative, output. Keep units consistent with engineering units, not raw ADC counts, once scaling is known.

Anti windup

When the actuator saturates, unbroken integration stores fantasy. On release, the output plunges through a long unwind. Anti windup pauses or back calculates the integral while saturated. Module 6 named the idea. Firmware must actually do it.

Timing hygiene

Use a periodic timer interrupt or a real time task with a measured period. Log overruns. If USB printing or blocking I2C steals milliseconds, your plant sees a different controller than the one you tuned. Watchdog timers catch deadlocks. They do not fix a poorly scheduled loop.

Worked scenario · OLED inside the control interrupt

A student tunes a DC motor position loop at 1 kHz on the bench. They then add a blocking OLED update inside the control interrupt. Sample jitter grows. The motor sings. Gains look unstable. Moving display updates to a slow task restores the earlier behaviour without changing Kp, Ki, or Kd.

Studio lab idea Implement discrete PID on any MCU you already use in Module 5. Command a step reference. Plot response at 100 Hz and at 1 kHz sample rates with the same gains. Record how overshoot and noise change. Add a saturating output limit and compare with and without anti windup.

Key terms

Sample period
Fixed time between control updates on an embedded target.
Anti windup
Logic that stops integral fantasy during actuator saturation.
Jitter
Variation in actual sample timing versus the planned period.
Watchdog
Timer that resets a stuck controller; it does not fix scheduling design.
Derivative filtering
Smoothing so differentiated noise does not thrash the actuator.
