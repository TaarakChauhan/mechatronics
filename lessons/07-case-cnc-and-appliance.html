Module 7 — Deep integrated systems

Case studies: CNC & appliance

Two more integrated systems: precision motion (CNC) and cost-optimised appliance control (inverter washer / robot vacuum patterns).

CNC axis loop

G-code → interpolator generates coordinated setpoints for X/Y/Z (and rotary) axes. Each axis: position loop → velocity loop → current loop on a servo drive. Feedback from rotary encoders or linear scales. Disturbances include cutting forces and friction. Tuning balances tracking lag vs chatter sensitivity. Look-ahead smoothing prevents corners from demanding impossible acceleration.

Modern appliance — inverter washer

Load sensing (motor current / unbalance), water level and temperature loops, and a BLDC or induction drum motor on an inverter. Sequence control runs wash phases; continuous control regulates speed through spin-up while limiting vibration. Fault modes: unbalanced load, blocked pump, heater dry-out — each needs detection and safe response.

Robot vacuums add mapping (LiDAR/vision), cliff IR sensors, bumper switches, and battery management — a mobile mechatronic product with localisation and coverage planning atop humble wheel motors.

Worked example — axis tracking lag

A velocity loop approximated as first-order with τ = 5 ms follows a ramp. Position lag under constant speed v accumulates roughly related to velocity-loop bandwidth and any proportional-only position gains. Designers add feedforward (velocity/acceleration) so the feedback loop only corrects model error — classic mechatronic move.

Key idea Cascaded loops (current ⊂ velocity ⊂ position) assign bandwidths an octave or more apart so inner loops look like actuators to outer loops.

Key terms

Interpolator
Generates time-based multi-axis setpoints from path geometry.
Cascaded control
Nested loops with decreasing bandwidth outward.
Inverter
Power electronics synthesising variable-frequency motor voltages.

Cost pressure versus precision

CNC spends money on scales, stiffness, and thermal compensation because micrometres pay. Appliances spend money on inverter reliability, unbalance algorithms, and UX because unit volume is huge and service calls are expensive. Both are mechatronics; the optimisation target differs.

Robot vacuums show how localisation and planning sit atop modest actuation — a preview of Module 8’s software stacks. Cliff sensors are simple IR proximity with serious safety meaning: false negatives drop robots downstairs.

Thermal and vibrational coupling

CNC accuracy drifts with spindle and ambient temperature; compensation tables are mechatronic software living on thermal models. Appliances manage vibration by speed profiles and mechanical dampers.

Both systems show cascaded control and sequential logic intertwined. Study them as templates for your own hybrid designs.

Serviceability differs: CNC expects skilled techs; appliances expect consumer UX and remote diagnostics. Diagnostics design follows the audience.

Energy efficiency regulations push inverter adoption in appliances — policy as a mechatronic design driver.

Extended studio notes — 07 case cnc and appliance

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Bandwidth separation in cascaded loops is how both precision and appliances stay calm. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming thermal steady state during the first hour of CNC cutting yields morning scrap. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Unbalance algorithms in washers are vibration mechatronics under cost constraints. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Service UX differs, but both need fault codes that mean something. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Inverters and FOC return in Module 8 as the new normal. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

Position loop 1 kHz, velocity 4 kHz, current 16 kHz — octave-ish separation. If someone sets all three to 16 kHz without authority, expect interacting tunings and noise. Restore separation.
