Module 2 — Sensing the physical world

Sensors and transducers

A sensor detects a physical quantity. A transducer converts energy from one form to another; many sensors are transducers that produce an electrical signal. In practice the words blur, but the engineering focus is clear: how faithfully does the electrical (or digital) output represent the measurand?

Static performance vocabulary

  • Range — the span of values the device can measure (e.g. 0–100 bar).
  • Span — often the algebraic difference between upper and lower range values.
  • Accuracy — closeness to the true value (includes bias and other errors), often stated as % of full scale or % of reading.
  • Precision / repeatability — closeness of repeated measurements under unchanged conditions.
  • Resolution — smallest distinguishable change in output.
  • Sensitivity — change in output per change in input (slope).
  • Hysteresis — path dependence: rising vs falling input yields different outputs.
  • Dead band — a region of input change that produces no measurable output change.

Watch-out High resolution does not imply high accuracy. A 24-bit ADC on a drifting sensor yields precise nonsense.

Worked example — sensitivity and span

A pressure transducer outputs 0.5 V at 0 kPa and 4.5 V at 200 kPa. Span of output = 4.0 V. Sensitivity ≈ 4.0 V / 200 kPa = 0.02 V/kPa. If the true pressure is 50 kPa and the device reads as if 52 kPa, the error is 2 kPa (1% of full-scale range).

Dynamic specs that static datasheets hide

Bandwidth, rise time, and damping tell you whether a sensor can track the process. A pressure sensor rated ±0.1% FS may still ring or lag enough to destabilise a fast valve loop. Always ask: what is the sensor’s transfer function relative to the closed-loop bandwidth you want?

Environmental specs — temperature coefficients, IP rating, shock — often dominate field failures over laboratory accuracy. A precise sensor in the wrong enclosure is an unreliable transducer.

Key idea Write an error budget: bias + nonlinearity + hysteresis + noise + digitisation + temperature drift, combined sensibly (not always worst-case arithmetic sum). Design margins against that budget.

Specification sheets without self-deception

When comparing two sensors, normalise accuracy statements. “±0.1% of reading” and “±0.1% of full scale” differ wildly near zero. Ask for hysteresis, non-repeatability, and long-term drift separately. A single “accuracy” number is often a marketing blend.

Consider the loading effect: does the sensor disturb the measurand? A large flow meter pressure drop changes the process you hoped to observe. A stiff force sensor in a delicate assembly can alter the contact mechanics.

Plan calibration intervals from drift data, not superstition. If temperature coefficient is 0.02%/°C and the environment swings 30°C, you already know a major error term before the first laboratory calibration.

Document mounting orientation, cable length, and supply tolerance as part of the sensor identity. Field swaps that ignore these create “mystery offsets” blamed on software.

Extended studio notes — 02 sensors and transducers

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Accuracy without stated reference conditions is not a specification; it is advertising. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming resolution equals accuracy confuses ADC marketing with metrology. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Hysteresis in a pressure sensor can destabilise a tightly tuned loop near a setpoint that chatters across the dead band. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Calibration certificates belong in the digital twin of the asset, not a drawer. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Smart-sensor lessons in Module 8 still depend on these static and dynamic definitions. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

A load cell with 0.05% FS accuracy on a 1000 N range has a 0.5 N accuracy class statement. If you use only the first 50 N of range, that fixed error is 1% of reading — possibly unacceptable. Either re-range the sensor or accept the percent-of-reading reality.
