Module 1 — Foundations

What is mechatronics?

Mechatronics is the disciplined integration of mechanical engineering, electronics, control theory, and software so that a product behaves as one coherent system rather than a pile of bolted-on specialties. The word was coined to capture a design attitude: decide the mechanics, the sensing, the actuation, and the information flow together, early, and iteratively.

A classical definition emphasises concurrency. Instead of finishing a gearbox and then “adding electronics,” a mechatronic team asks how encoder resolution, motor torque ripple, structural stiffness, and control bandwidth constrain one another. The result is often a simpler mechanism driven by smarter sensing and software — or, conversely, a more precise mechanism that lets a modest controller meet the specification.

Key idea Mechatronics is not “mechanics plus a microcontroller.” It is concurrent design across energy domains and information, with feedback as the organising principle of behaviour.

The design process (overview)

A practical mechatronic design loop looks like this:

  1. Need and constraints — function, cost, environment, safety, standards.
  2. Physical principle — what energy conversion and sensing make the function possible.
  3. Architecture — sensors, actuators, plant, controller, human interface, communications.
  4. Models — lumped dynamics, signal ranges, power budgets, timing.
  5. Prototype and identify — measure real dynamics; refine the model.
  6. Control and software — closed-loop strategies, fault handling, diagnostics.
  7. Validate — against requirements, including disturbance and failure cases.

Measurement systems vs control systems

Two related but different jobs appear everywhere. A measurement system observes a quantity and presents or records it — for example a laboratory scale, a weather station, or a production line that only logs torque. A control system uses measurements (and a desired setpoint) to drive actuators so the plant follows a reference despite disturbances.

Many products are both: an anti-lock braking system measures wheel speeds and continuously modulates brake pressure. The measurement chain must be fast and trustworthy; the control law must respect actuator limits and safety.

Everyday examples

  • ABS / ESC — wheel-speed sensors, hydraulic modulators, and a stability controller that intervenes faster than a human can.
  • Camera autofocus / image stabilisation — contrast or phase-detect sensing, voice-coil or piezoelectric actuators, and predictive control of lens groups.
  • CNC machine tools — encoders or linear scales, servo drives, interpolators that turn G-code into coordinated axis motion.
  • Washing machine — load sensing, inverter-driven drum motor, water-level and temperature loops, and a sequenced programme of wash phases.
Then vs now Classical mechatronics texts centred on analogue sensors, brushed motors, and ladder-logic PLCs. In 2026 the same conceptual stack still holds, but the components are often MEMS sensors, FOC-driven BLDC motors, Ethernet-based fieldbuses, and edge AI for diagnostics — covered deeply in Module 8.

Worked example — choosing the integration point

A team must position a linear stage to ±0.05 mm under a 20 N cutting load. Option A: a stiff ball-screw with a coarse encoder and modest control bandwidth. Option B: a lighter belt drive with a fine linear encoder and higher-bandwidth servo. Mechatronic reasoning compares total error budget: mechanical compliance under load, encoder quantisation, sampling delay, and disturbance rejection. Often Option B wins on cost and mass if the control loop can close tightly; Option A wins if the environment is dirty and the encoder must live far from the tool. The “right” answer is an architecture decision, not a single-discipline preference.

Concurrent design
Deciding domain choices together so trade-offs are visible early.

Integration metrics and organisational reality

Teams that claim to practice mechatronics still fail when procurement buys the motor before the control bandwidth is known, or when software starts only after metal is cut. A practical metric is the date of the first joint architecture review that includes mechanics, electronics, controls, and software with a shared error budget spreadsheet. If that review never happens, you have a sequential project with a fashionable label.

Another metric is the number of cross-domain requirements that are testable: “position error under 20 N load at 0.5 m/s shall be ≤0.05 mm with 95% confidence” beats “high performance.” Testable requirements force sensor resolution, structural stiffness, and sample rate into the open.

Organisationally, mechatronics thrives when interface owners exist. Someone must own the connector pinout, the network cycle time, and the thermal path from driver to chassis. Diffused responsibility produces orphaned EMC problems and late cable redesigns.

Finally, remember that examples in this course — ABS, cameras, CNC, appliances — succeed because their industries institutionalised concurrent design. Your project can copy the components without copying the culture and still fail. Treat process as part of the product.

Extended studio notes — 01 what is mechatronics

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Cost, mass, precision, and schedule rarely peak together; concurrent design is the disciplined argument about which one yields. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. For example, assuming zero backlash in a cheap gearbox will haunt positioning accuracy reviews. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” ABS and appliance teams learned this decades ago; your prototype team can learn it cheaper by instrumenting early. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Interface control sheets for connectors and timing are as critical as CAD for brackets. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. Module 8 will not replace this lesson; it will wrap it in connectivity and assurance. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

Suppose two vendors quote “0.1 mm accuracy.” Vendor A means bidirectional repeatability without load; Vendor B means absolute accuracy under 50 N. A mechatronic specification table with load, speed, temperature, and statistical definition prevents buying the wrong quote.
