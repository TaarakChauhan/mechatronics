Module 2 — Sensing the physical world

Process sensors

Process industries and many mechatronic machines regulate pressure, flow, level, temperature, and sometimes light or chemical composition. The physics differs, but the measurement-chain discipline does not.

Pressure, flow, level

Pressure sensors use diaphragms with strain gauges, capacitive gaps, or piezoresistive MEMS. Flow may be inferred from differential pressure across an orifice, turbine spinning, ultrasonic transit time, or thermal mass flow. Level uses hydrostatic pressure, floats, radar, ultrasonic time-of-flight, or capacitive probes.

Temperature

  • RTD (e.g. Pt100) — resistance rises nearly linearly with temperature; accurate and stable.
  • Thermocouple — junction EMF from dissimilar metals; wide range, needs cold-junction compensation.
  • Thermistor — strongly nonlinear resistance (usually NTC); high sensitivity in a limited band.

Light

Photodiodes and phototransistors convert irradiance to current/voltage; used in encoders, presence detection, colour marks, and flame sensing. Spectral response and ambient light rejection matter.

Key idea Match sensor dynamics to the process: a fast flame detector and a slow kiln thermocouple are both “temperature-related” but live in different bandwidth worlds.

Worked example — RTD reading

A Pt100 is approximately 100.0 Ω at 0 °C and 138.5 Ω at 100 °C. Measured R = 119.4 Ω. Linear interpolation: T ≈ 100 × (119.4−100)/(138.5−100) ≈ 50.4 °C. Real designs use Callendar–Van Dusen polynomials and lead-wire compensation (3-wire/4-wire).

Then vs now 4–20 mA analogue transmitters still dominate brownfield plants; IO-Link and Ethernet-APL smart sensors add identity, diagnostics, and dual-channel safety data (Module 8).

Installation effects

A thermocouple in a thermowell measures the well more than the fluid until heat transfer catches up — another lag in series with the process. Orifice plates need straight pipe runs; swirl ruins ΔP inference. Ultrasonic level fails with foam; radar may cope better. The “sensor” includes its mechanical installation.

For temperature loops, match sensor mass to the needed speed. A fine-wire thermocouple responds quickly but is fragile; a heavy RTD in a thick well is slow and robust. Mechatronic design picks the pair that meets both dynamics and reliability.

Process dynamics and sensor choice

Dead time in pipes and thermowells dominates many loops. Prefer sensors and placements that shrink dead time before buying a fancier controller. Moving a temperature sensor upstream of a long pipe can outperform months of PID tuning.

Differential pressure level on closed tanks needs dry/wet leg design; temperature changes in the leg create offsets. Guided-wave radar avoids some of those issues at higher cost.

Thermocouple types (J, K, T, …) trade range and sensitivity; never mix alloys in extension cables. Cold-junction compensation errors of a few degrees are common when CJC sensors sit in hot cabinets.

Light sensors in industrial settings fight ambient sunlight and weld flash. Modulation and synchronous detection are conditioning topics that belong with the sensor selection, not as later surprises.

Extended studio notes — 02 process sensors

In professional mechatronics work, the ideas in this lesson show up less as isolated definitions and more as constraints that fight each other. Fluid and thermal plants are slow, but their dead times are deadly for aggressive tuning. When you write requirements, force numbers onto those constraints: sample rates, newton-metres, degrees Celsius, milliseconds of latency, millimetres of error. Vague adjectives hide trade-offs until tooling steel is already ordered.

A useful studio habit is to keep a living “assumption list” beside your block diagram. Typical assumptions include linearity, constant temperature, perfect sampling clocks, infinite actuator authority, and noise-free sensors. Assuming the thermowell is invisible adds seconds of lag you will blame on the controller. Crossing out an assumption should trigger either a model upgrade, a hardware change, or an explicit residual risk acceptance signed by someone accountable.

Commissioning is where theory meets cable trays. Expect ground loops, mis-labelled I/O, incorrect engineering-unit scales, and mechanical binding that looks like a “software bug.” Flow inferences from ΔP fail when Reynolds numbers leave the calibrated regime. Bring an oscilloscope or drive trace tool early; bring a torque wrench and alignment dial indicator just as early. The mechatronic engineer who only debugs in an IDE will misclassify half of field faults.

Documentation is part of the control system. Record firmware hashes, gain sets, mechanical revisions, and network topologies together. Future failure analysis needs that bundle. Installation drawings are part of the sensor datasheet in practice. If your organisation treats documents as after-the-fact paperwork, expect to rediscover tuning constants from scratch after every staff rotation.

Finally, connect this lesson forward and backward in the course. Foundations without modern networks leave you unable to ship connected machines; modern buzzwords without foundations leave you unable to stabilise a simple loop. IO-Link diagnostics later will report dirty electrodes — physics remains first. Use the quizzes not as memorisation theatre but as prompts to redraw the diagrams from memory on paper — that retrieval practice builds the intuition you will need on a noisy factory floor.

Additional worked scenario

An RTD in a 6 mm thermowell shows τ ≈ 8 s in flowing water tests. The process fluid τ is 40 s. The combined first-order lag is not 40 s; the well adds a second lag. Identify both or your Smith predictor will be aimed at a fiction.
